## Executive summary

Ireland runs one of the most wind-penetrated power systems in the world, but a growing share of that wind is never used. This report catalogues every publicly accessible tool, dataset, API, and document relevant to **modelling and reducing wind curtailment on Ireland's high-voltage transmission grid**, organized into five categories: grid topology, curtailment/constraint data, real-time and historical system data, wind resource data, and prior research. For each source it gives the access method, format, update frequency, and why it matters. It closes with the highest-value starting points for a time-boxed build, the real data gaps nobody has solved yet, and sources that turned out to be dead ends.

## Motivation: why this problem matters

Ireland's transmission system operator, EirGrid (working jointly with SONI in Northern Ireland), must keep the all-island grid stable in real time while accommodating as much wind and solar generation as it safely can. When available renewable output exceeds what the system can absorb, EirGrid instructs wind and solar farms to reduce output — a process it calls **dispatch-down**, split into two distinct phenomena:

- **Curtailment** — system-wide dispatch-down, driven mainly by the **System Non-Synchronous Penetration (SNSP)** limit (the maximum share of non-synchronous generation, e.g. wind, solar and HVDC imports, the system can carry at any instant) and by **MUON** (the requirement to keep a Minimum number of conventional Units ONline for frequency, voltage, and reserve stability).
- **Constraint** — localized dispatch-down, driven by insufficient transmission capacity in a specific part of the network (a "traffic jam" on the grid), independent of the system-wide SNSP position.

The two require fundamentally different optimization approaches — SNSP/MUON curtailment is addressed by system-level flexibility (storage, demand response, interconnection, faster unit ramping), while constraint is addressed by network-level solutions (reinforcement, redispatch, or siting new generation/storage away from congested nodes). Any optimization tool needs to model them separately.

The scale of the problem is real and growing:

- Ireland and Northern Ireland sourced **40.0% of electricity demand from renewables in 2024**, with 13,288 GWh of wind energy generated across the island that year.
- The **SNSP limit has been raised from 50% (2015) to 65% (2018) to 70% (2021) to its current 75% (since April 2022)**, with EirGrid and SONI targeting **95% by 2030** to meet Ireland's 80%-renewable-electricity-by-2030 policy target.
- Independent industry analysis found **MUON, not SNSP, was responsible for roughly 80–96% of curtailment** in the years studied — a system operated at a headline "75% SNSP limit" is still losing most of its curtailed wind to a different, less-discussed constraint entirely.
- `[CORRECTED 01-09-26]` ~~Constraint costs alone were estimated at €567 million in 2024/25, forecast to rise to €700 million in 2025/26~~ — **these are not "constraint costs."** €567.21m is the **Network Imperfections Charge allowed for tariff year 2024/25** (SEM Committee decision **SEM-25-053**), which also covers make-whole payments and net imbalance energy cost. For 2025/26 the TSOs proposed **€883.24m** (€22.28/MWh) and the SEM Committee disallowed €93m; the "€700m" figure is a rounding of the base forecast, not the submitted number. In 2025 the Republic of Ireland's wind dispatch-down rate reached roughly **11.3%** (4.7% curtailment + 6.6% constraint), meaning more than one in every ten megawatt-hours of available wind energy went unused. All-island 2024 was 14.0%; Northern Ireland alone 29.6%.
- Every megawatt-hour of curtailed wind is a megawatt-hour of near-zero-marginal-cost clean energy displaced, typically by gas generation held online to satisfy MUON or local network limits — directly undermining Ireland's climate targets even as installed wind capacity keeps growing.

This is why the problem is a natural hackathon target: it sits at the intersection of publicly available real-time grid data, a well-documented (if imperfectly harmonized) regulatory dataset, and a clear, quantifiable cost — both financial and in tonnes of avoidable CO₂ — that a good optimization or decision-support tool could plausibly move the needle on.

## Scope and approach

This report was compiled through targeted web research (30+ searches and page fetches) across five categories requested for the project: **(1)** grid topology and infrastructure, **(2)** curtailment and constraint data specifically, **(3)** real-time and historical system data, **(4)** wind resource and farm location data, and **(5)** existing research and prior art. Every source below was checked for whether it is real, currently accessible, and machine-readable enough to build against within a hackathon timeframe; sources that failed those checks are listed separately in the "dead ends" section rather than silently omitted.

## Highest-value starting points given hackathon time constraints

0. `[ADDED 01-09-26]` **Download the Irish network model.** One command, 20 MB, CC BY 4.0, no signup, no API key, no waiting on anyone:
   ```
   curl -L -o ireland_case_july2025.xlsx https://zenodo.org/records/17287498/files/ireland_case_july2025.xlsx
   ```
   446 buses, 586 lines, 183 transformers, 288 generators. This is the asset that everything else on this list supports.
1. **`Daniel-Parke/EirGrid_Data_Download`** (GitHub) — run this early. In ~11 minutes (async version) you get years of demand/generation/wind/SNSP/CO₂/interconnector history in CSV, with zero API key friction. This is your ground truth for validating anything the network model produces.
2. **EirGrid's "System and Renewable Data Reports" hub** (https://www.eirgrid.ie/grid/system-and-renewable-data-reports) — the `DD-Summary-Report` xlsx alone gives you a half-hourly, multi-year curtailment/constraint time series without any scraping at all.
3. **arXiv:2608.24464** (the Aug 2026 MILP paper) — read §V and §V-B closely; it's effectively a data-sourcing and modelling guide for exactly this problem, four days old, from an Irish network specifically.
4. **SEAI's `WindFarmsConnectedJune2022.csv`** + **Open-Meteo's free ERA5 API** — together these give you, with almost no setup cost, a per-farm location/capacity table joined to decades of hourly modelled wind speed at each site. This is probably your fastest path to a "potential vs actual output" curtailment estimate.
5. **`energy-modelling-ireland.github.io/wiki`** — keep this open as a live reference throughout the hackathon; if you hit a data need not covered above, check here before spending time searching further.

## Real gaps — nothing publicly available was found for these

- **STILL A GAP — a machine-readable map of which named "constraint group" each wind farm belongs to.** EirGrid's Wind Dispatch Tool Constraint Group Overview PDF names the ~39 groups and explains the logic, but publishes membership at **transmission-station level only** — there is no farm→group lookup table. `[CORRECTED 01-09-26]` The suggested workaround below (correlate which farms get curtailed together) is now second-best: since the network model is public, you can instead **compute** each farm's effectiveness against each overload directly, which is EirGrid's own stated definition of group membership. Correlation remains a useful independent check on that calculation.
- ~~**A full, open bus/branch network model with impedances and thermal ratings** for DC-OPF-style optimization.~~ **CLOSED `[CORRECTED 01-09-26]`.** Ref [27] turned out to be public after all: **"Ireland Electricity System Model", Zenodo [10.5281/zenodo.17287498](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17287498), CC BY 4.0, 446 buses / 586 lines / 183 transformers / 288 generators.** The TYTFS "Study Files" (PSS/E-format) are no longer your only option. Caveat: the author labels it a draft needing validation, and the accompanying paper notes N-1 security is not modelled, so it underestimates constraint.
- **Day-ahead or real-time SNSP forecasts as an open dataset.** EirGrid publishes the current SNSP series and a couple of one-off Wind Forecasting Accuracy Reports, but no live day-ahead SNSP forecast feed exists publicly — a 2024 ScienceDirect paper had to build its own forecasting model precisely because this doesn't exist.
- **ESB Networks' full distribution network geometry (CAD data)** is explicitly "available upon request" (FOI) only, not publicly downloadable — the capacity heatmap is a reasonable substitute but is a coarse A–F categorical rating, not full topology.
- **A live/near-real-time constraint (as opposed to curtailment) breakdown by cause.** The published annual and monthly reports are retrospective; there's no public feed distinguishing *which specific constraint* is binding at a given moment, only after-the-fact aggregates.

## Sources that looked promising but turned out to be dead ends, paywalled, or too coarse

- **Wind Energy Ireland / IWEA member project map** — no public downloadable database was found; a third-party "Ireland Wind Farms Database 2024" exists but is a **paid** ResearchAndMarkets.com report, not a free/open dataset.
- **Heritage Council "Wind Farms" dataset on data.gov.ie** — the listed resource is an internal ArcSDE database connection string, not an actual downloadable file; use the SEAI wind-farms CSV instead (section 1/4), which is more current and genuinely downloadable.
- **ESB Networks CAD network data** — confirmed to exist but is FOI/request-only, not open data; don't budget hackathon time trying to obtain it.
- **ENTSO-E API** — not a dead end, but the registration-plus-email-for-token step is **not instant**; if you plan to use it, request access in the first hour of the hackathon, not when you actually need the data.
- **OpenInfraMap full-country bulk export** — the free route (Overpass Turbo) works but is rate-limited and manual for large queries; the polished bulk export is a paid product (Infrageomatics) — fine for a quick GeoJSON pull, not for repeated large-scale automation.

## Conclusion

Ireland's curtailment problem is unusually well-suited to a data-driven hackathon project: the core time series (dispatch-down volumes, system demand, wind generation, SNSP) are published in accessible spreadsheet form or scrapeable via an existing open-source downloader, the underlying physics and regulatory drivers are documented in detail by EirGrid's own reports and recent academic work, and the financial and climate stakes are large and easy to communicate to judges. The main technical risk is not data *availability* but data *integration* — joining generator locations to network nodes to constraint groups to the correct half-hourly dispatch-down figures is fiddly, and no single official source does this end-to-end. Teams should budget time for that join rather than assuming it falls out of any one dataset, and should treat the August 2026 arXiv MILP paper and the `EirGrid_Data_Download` GitHub repo as the fastest route to a working baseline before attempting anything more novel.
